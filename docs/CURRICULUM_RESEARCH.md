# GuitarLab Curriculum Research and Revision

**Research date:** 2026-11-11  
**Research method:** Exa deep research; 8 distinct workstreams, 72 search results screened, followed by full-page review of selected research, university curricula, guitar syllabi and pedagogy resources.  
**Decision:** Retain the 52-week core sequence and its stable week IDs, but revise the teaching model from a mostly week-by-week topic list into a spiral, integrated musicianship curriculum.

## 1. Executive findings

The existing Year 1 and Year 2 routes are broad and contain a strong mix of chords, rhythm guitar, lead technique, fretboard, fingerstyle, blues, harmony, ear learning and performance. The strongest improvement is not to replace all 104 weeks. It is to make key musicianship skills recur from the beginning, improve the prerequisites of a few exercises, and define how a learner practises and demonstrates each skill.

The research and authoritative curricula converge on these principles:

1. **Practise with a specific goal, feedback and reflection.** Practice time is not the same as deliberate practice. The music-practice literature repeatedly studies goal setting, strategies, metacognition, self-regulation and self-evaluation. Use a loop of plan → attempt → listen/observe → adjust → reflect, rather than a passive timer. [How et al., PRISMA review](https://journals.sagepub.com/doi/10.1177/10298649211005531); [Platz et al., meta-analysis](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC4073287/); [Varela et al., systematic review](https://doi.org/10.1177/0305735614554639).

2. **Teach integrated musicianship, not isolated guitar mechanics.** Berklee's beginner guitar course explicitly integrates musicianship, technique, tone, reading and repertoire; its outcomes include fretboard awareness, chord/strumming skills, improvisation, theory, learning songs and playing by ear. A guitar pedagogy thesis argues for early integration, pattern recognition, sequencing and metacognition. [Berklee Guitar for Beginners](https://online.berklee.edu/courses/guitar-for-beginners); [Cummings, Revamping the Guitar Curriculum](https://digitalcommons.csumb.edu/caps_thes_all/952); [Steadman, An integrated approach to beginning music-making on the classical guitar](http://hdl.handle.net/2152/954).

3. **Keep pulse and listening alive from Week 1.** Do not postpone ear work until Year 1 Week 37 or concentrate it only in Year 2 Weeks 29–32. Every new musical idea can be heard, sung/hummed, played and then named. Rhythm can be felt, chanted, tapped and played before notation is introduced. Gordon Institute of Music Learning resources provide a coherent audiation-based approach, though these principles should be treated as a pedagogical framework, not proof that one method is uniquely superior for every adult guitar learner. [GIML: Specific applications](https://giml.org/mlt/applications/); [GIML: Teaching procedures](https://giml.org/mlt/lsa-teaching/).

4. **Use both blocked and mixed practice, at the right time.** Focused repetition can help a learner establish a new movement; later, mix it with related tasks and musical contexts to practise retrieval and transfer. Evidence for interleaving in music is not uniform: one clarinet study reported advantages for interleaving in some outcomes, while a 2024 study with advanced violinists found blocked practice more effective at 24-hour retention. Therefore, GuitarLab should not enforce random interleaving as a universal rule. Begin with a small focused block, then mix familiar skills when the learner is ready. [Carter & Grahn, music practice schedules](https://ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/pmc/articles/PMC4989027/); [Mathias & Goldman, 2024](https://doi.org/10.1177/00224294231222801).

5. **Review skills after a delay and adapt the interval.** Retrieval after some forgetting can strengthen retention. An adaptive-spacing study found better retention for adaptive spacing than random schedules under its tested conditions. Use confidence, accuracy and response latency as signals when available, but don't pretend one universal interval schedule has been validated for all guitar skills. [Mettler et al., adaptive spacing](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC8324179/).

6. **Assess more than speed.** Music-performance assessment systems commonly include technical and expressive/musical criteria, but assessment can be subjective. Use a transparent exercise-specific rubric: correct notes/chords, sound quality, pulse/rhythm, relaxed control and musical application. Keep measured evidence separate from learner self-report. [Moura et al., systematic review of solo performance assessment](https://www.frontiersin.org/journals/psychology/articles/10.3389/fpsyg.2024.1467434/full); [McPherson, Blackwell & Hattie, feedback in music performance teaching](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC9251491/).

7. **Make physical comfort a recurring skill.** Guitar posture and playing-related musculoskeletal discomfort are documented concerns. Build instrument setup, relaxed movement, breaks and a stop-if-pain rule into beginner instruction. The app must not diagnose pain or prescribe rehabilitation. [Portnoy et al., PLOS ONE](https://journals.plos.org/plosone/article?id=10.1371%2Fjournal.pone.0262207); [University of Iowa guitar health considerations](https://iowaprotocols.medicine.uiowa.edu/protocols/guitar-playing-part-1-health-considerations-music-and-medicine-monday).

8. **A rounded guitar curriculum assesses several strands.** College and graded guitar syllabi include chords, scales/arpeggios, rhythm, lead playing, aural skills, reading, repertoire and performance—not just chord shapes. This supports GuitarLab's breadth, while reinforcing the need to spiral the strands. [Rochester Community and Technical College beginning guitar outline](https://www.rctc.edu/files/ccos/MUSC-1431-CCO.html); [London College of Music electric guitar exams](https://lcme.uwl.ac.uk/our-exams/guitar-ukulele/electric-guitar); [Trinity Rock & Pop syllabus](https://www.trinityrock.com/exams/syllabus).

## 2. Audit of the existing sequence

### Strengths to preserve

- The 52-week Year 1 and 52-week Year 2 route is broad and progresses from foundations to intermediate musicianship.
- The Year 1 sequence already contains open chords, strumming, picking, fingerstyle, scales, shell chords, blues, power/barre chords, triads, improvisation, ear learning, composition and performance.
- The Year 2 route adds fretboard mapping, CAGED, triad inversions, rhythm guitar, scales, blues, fingerstyle, transcription, harmony, jazz and independent musicianship.
- Four optional specialisations allow a learner to choose a direction without making all advanced genres mandatory.
- Weekly checkpoints and a progress-based pace are preferable to a rigid calendar deadline.

### Gaps to correct

| Finding | Why it matters | Revision |
|---|---|---|
| Ear learning is concentrated late in Year 1 and Year 2 | Listening, singing and internal hearing should support chord, rhythm and melody learning from the beginning | Add a small hear/sing/play task to every week |
| Rhythm is introduced as a dedicated topic only at Week 7 | Pulse and subdivision are foundational to all playing | Add pulse, tapping and counting to every session from Week 1 |
| Week 6 chord loop uses C before the dedicated C chord week (Week 9) | This creates a prerequisite mismatch | Change the loop to chords already introduced: Em–G–D–A |
| Early fretboard checkpoint asks for natural notes across all six strings | The scope is too broad for a first map | Make Year 1 Week 11 a landmark/interval introduction; deepen note mapping in Year 2 |
| Practice structure can be interpreted as a mandatory 40-minute daily block | Time spent is not a mastery measure and can discourage short sessions | Provide 10/20/40-minute plans and custom duration |
| Tempo markers could be misread as pass marks | Starting BPM is not a universal proficiency standard | Keep BPM as a starting suggestion; measure clean accuracy separately |
| Reviews are present but not clearly spaced or adaptive | One-off tests do not show durable retention | Use low-friction retrieval checks across later sessions |
| Physical comfort is not prominent enough in weekly practice | Poor setup, tension and overuse can undermine practice | Include relaxed setup, breaks and stop-if-pain guidance |
| Weekly topic records were outlines, not full lessons | A title and checkpoint are not sufficient teaching content | Author full lesson blocks, exercises, worked examples and checks; now completed for 156 curriculum entries |

## 3. Revised instructional model

Use a **spiral + whole-part-whole** model:

- **Whole:** listen to a short musical example or play a simple phrase so the learner knows what the skill is for.
- **Part:** isolate one manageable movement, note group, chord shape or rhythm.
- **Whole:** put the skill back into a progression, melody, song section or improvisation.

Every week remains centred on its current topic, but five strands recur:

1. **Sound and rhythm:** pulse, count, clap/tap, sing/hum, listen and play.
2. **Technique and tone:** relaxed setup, fretting/picking control, clean sound and efficient movement.
3. **Fretboard and theory:** note/interval/chord relationships taught through actual guitar positions.
4. **Repertoire and creativity:** apply the new skill to a real song, groove, melody or original phrase.
5. **Reflection and retrieval:** recall without looking, record a result and choose the next target.

Not every strand needs equal time in every session. The point is regular contact and transfer, not cramming five subjects into a short practice.

## 4. Specific curriculum revisions

The existing weekly IDs remain stable. The first content revision makes these targeted changes:

### Year 1

- **Week 1:** add a pulse and listening task to the open-string routine. Introduce relaxed posture and a brief comfort check.
- **Weeks 2–7:** keep pulse and ear tasks recurring. Use the chord shapes already taught in each progression.
- **Week 6:** replace Em–G–D–C with **Em–G–D–A**, so every chord has already been introduced by Weeks 4–5.
- **Weeks 8, 13 and 14:** preserve staff notation as an optional parallel track; TAB remains the core route. If the learner chooses notation, keep a short recurring reading activity rather than teaching isolated notation weeks and then expecting it to persist.
- **Week 11:** narrow the first fretboard checkpoint to open-string names, semitone/fret relationships and a small number of landmarks. Deeper natural-note mapping belongs in Year 2.
- **Weeks 15–23:** keep focused technique work; use slow, clean repetitions before increasing tempo. Make comfort and efficient pressure explicit.
- **Weeks 19–23:** connect blues form, seventh chords, power chords and minor pentatonic through one musical context, rather than treating each as a disconnected topic.
- **Weeks 25–36:** link roots, octaves, triads, keys, chord tones and scale phrases; continue ear and rhythm review.
- **Weeks 37–52:** retain ear learning, songwriting, blues, jazz, sight-reading and performance, but treat them as a deeper application of skills already encountered—not the first exposure to listening or musical creativity.

### Year 2

- **Weeks 1–6:** deepen fretboard mapping and triads with accurate recall, interval naming and chord-tone hearing.
- **Weeks 7–11:** rhythm-guitar work should include pulse, muting, subdivisions, starts/stops and dynamics—not only chord shape speed.
- **Weeks 12–16:** connect scale positions to roots, chord tones, singing and short phrases.
- **Weeks 17–22:** preserve the blues sequence; bends should be matched to a fretted target pitch at a comfortable intensity.
- **Weeks 23–28:** retain fingerstyle and arranging; make thumb independence, melody/accompaniment balance and relaxed movement observable.
- **Weeks 29–34:** keep the dedicated repertoire/ear block, but build on weekly listening and transcription micro-tasks from Year 1.
- **Weeks 35–45:** connect intervals, seventh chords, extensions, substitutions and jazz to sound and voice leading on the fretboard.
- **Weeks 46–52:** preserve independent practice design, transcription, solo writing, recording and a three-piece performance capstone.

### Year 3+

Keep the four specialisation pathways. Entry should be based on demonstrated prerequisite skills and learner interest, not merely reaching a date or finishing a fixed number of calendar weeks. Each path should have a shared musicianship thread: listening, time, technique, repertoire, creativity and reflection.

## 5. Practice design and progression rules

### A flexible session

Use 40 minutes as a suggested full session, not a requirement. Support 10- and 20-minute alternatives:

- **10 minutes:** one priority skill, a brief musical application and a quick result.
- **20 minutes:** warm-up/comfort check, focused skill, musical application and short reflection.
- **40 minutes:** warm-up, focused skill, review, new learning, musical application and reflection.

A learner may use a custom duration. A timer records elapsed time only.

### How a new skill should be practised

1. Hear or see a simple example.
2. State one specific goal.
3. Work slowly on one small part.
4. Observe one thing (accuracy, sound, pulse, tension or movement).
5. Make one adjustment.
6. Repeat until the movement is reasonably stable.
7. Mix it with one previously learned skill.
8. Apply it to music.
9. Return to it after a delay.

### Tempo progression

- Begin at a tempo where the learner can play comfortably and accurately.
- Increase only after the exercise-specific quality target is met.
- Record actual BPM and clean repetitions separately from total attempts.
- If quality falls, return to a manageable tempo.
- No universal clean-streak or BPM target should be treated as mastery.

### Review scheduling

Use adaptive review, not a rigid streak:
- schedule a review sooner after errors, hesitation or low confidence;
- lengthen the interval after accurate, confident retrieval on separate occasions;
- periodically ask the learner to recall a skill without showing its diagram first;
- bring a stable skill back in a new musical context.

The first implementation can use a transparent heuristic. Do not claim it is a scientifically validated optimum.

## 6. Assessment and mastery rubric

Each exercise defines its own success criteria. Use the relevant dimensions, not one generic score:

| Dimension | What to observe |
|---|---|
| Accuracy | Correct note, string, fret, chord tones, rhythm or pattern |
| Sound | Clear tone, intended strings, controlled noise and articulation |
| Time | Pulse continuity, subdivision and alignment with a metronome/backing track |
| Control | Relaxed, repeatable movement without unnecessary pressure |
| Musical use | Skill applied to a progression, phrase, song section or improvisation |
| Understanding | Learner can explain, sing, name or recall the idea where appropriate |

Evidence labels:
- **Measured:** a validated tool measured the specific property.
- **Observed/self-checked:** learner completed a defined task and checked it.
- **Self-reported:** learner confidence or difficulty rating.
- **Reviewed recording:** learner or trusted teacher reviewed a recording.

Do not convert a microphone signal into an objective grade unless the detector has been validated for that task and instrument. A tuner can measure pitch of a sufficiently isolated note; it cannot infer all aspects of technique or musicality.

A reasonable default mastery rule is more than one successful demonstration separated in time, with at least one application outside the isolated drill. The learner can revisit or override the status. Treat this as a transparent product rule, not a universal research-derived threshold.

## 7. Physical comfort and accessibility

- Show a concise setup reminder: stable instrument support, relaxed shoulders, neutral wrist as practical, minimal necessary finger pressure.
- Use short warm-up and rest opportunities; never encourage practising through pain, numbness or tingling.
- If pain or persistent symptoms occur, stop the exercise and seek advice from an appropriately qualified health professional. GuitarLab is not a medical tool.
- Support right- and left-handed display, note-name preferences, adjustable tempo, reduced motion, captions/transcripts for video references and keyboard-accessible controls.
- Do not make a single posture or technique image the only acceptable physical solution; instrument size, body and playing context vary.

## 8. Content authoring standard

A teachable lesson should contain:
- learner-facing objectives;
- a short explanation with guitar-specific examples;
- a demonstration/reference link with access notes;
- at least one focused drill;
- one musical application;
- exercise-specific success criteria;
- a common mistake and correction cue;
- an accessible/slower alternative;
- a retrieval prompt and next-review suggestion.

Write original explanations and exercises. Link to the learner's paid course and external references; do not copy protected course text, videos, books or tabs.

## 9. Research limitations

- Much practice research concerns classical musicians, piano, strings or advanced performers rather than adult self-taught beginner guitarists. Translate principles carefully.
- Findings on interleaved versus blocked music practice are mixed; use learner progress and task complexity to choose.
- A systematic review or university syllabus can inform curriculum design, but it does not prove that one exact 52-week order is optimal.
- The recommended review intervals, mastery rules and session presets are product heuristics to test with real learner experience.
- The curriculum should be revised from observed learning evidence after the app is used, not treated as immutable.

## 11. Revision policy

- Preserve all current Year 1 and Year 2 week IDs.
- Keep curriculum content completion separate from evidence-based skill mastery; do not import personal lesson continuity.
- Treat this research document as the rationale for the curriculum framework.
- Revise weekly lesson content in small, versioned increments; preserve curriculum IDs and never infer imported learner history.
- Validate all curriculum JSON and cross-references in the curriculum-specific GitHub Actions workflow; extend coverage into application CI during M1.
