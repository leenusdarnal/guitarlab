# M7.2 — Evidence-Based Curriculum and Assessment Improvement Review

**Research status:** Multi-angle literature and product scan completed; recommendations below are ready for controlled implementation  
**Repository baseline:** 34 skill identifiers, 12 catalogue categories, 152 authored lessons  
**Current work branch:** `feat/m7-skill-catalog-adaptive-practice`  
**Data-change policy:** No skill IDs, lesson mappings, learner assessments, or mastery records are changed by this research review.

## 1. Executive summary

The next meaningful improvement is not to expand the taxonomy. GuitarLab already has a usable 34-skill catalogue and extensive authored lesson content. The more important gap is that a skill's existence and a lesson's mapping do not yet guarantee a valid, observable, level-appropriate assessment.

This review recommends a distinct assessment-profile layer for the existing skill IDs, backed by a plan–practice–reflect learning cycle and by retention/transfer checks. The profiles should remain draft until curriculum-owner review. They should not be used to award mastery, numeric scores, or automated pass/fail outcomes yet.

The evidence converges on four practical directions:

1. Teach learners how to plan, monitor and reflect on practice rather than only prescribing repetitions.
2. Use skill-specific observable criteria and performance rubrics, not one generic checkpoint for every kind of musical outcome.
3. Distinguish immediate practice performance from retained learning; include delayed and varied-context probes.
4. Preserve the broader musical processes of creating, performing, responding and connecting, rather than reducing progress to technique scores.

## 2. Scope and research approach

Research was organized into five distinct angles:

1. Music practice and self-regulated learning.
2. Performance-assessment validity, reliability and rubric design.
3. Motor learning, feedback timing, practice variability, retention and transfer.
4. Curriculum progression and music education standards.
5. Public guitar-learning products, used only to observe product patterns—not to establish educational efficacy.

Peer-reviewed systematic reviews and empirical studies were weighted above vendor marketing. Official music-education standards were treated as professional frameworks, not experimental proof. Commercial product pages were used only for patterns such as staged paths, guided practice, expert review, and visible milestones.

## 3. Evidence synthesis

### 3.1 Self-regulated learning should be a taught product capability

Music practice research describes a recurring cycle: plan the task and set a goal; perform while monitoring the result; reflect, evaluate and adapt the next attempt. A 2025 scoping review of pedagogical approaches in instrumental learning identified 17 qualifying studies from 934 records. It found that both explicit self-regulation instruction and tools such as practice journals, e-portfolios and digital applications can foster self-regulation; it also identified a lack of long-term evidence and research in online/hybrid contexts.

A 2025 systematic review of 31 music studies (seven included in meta-analysis) identified four mechanisms: structured learning support, strategy instruction, technology-enhanced feedback and teacher support. The authors reported a medium positive overall meta-analytic effect, while noting variation by educational stage and the need for better task-aligned measures.

**GuitarLab implication:** Practice Studio should eventually prompt learners to set a small task-specific target, choose a strategy, log what happened, and decide what to change next. A raw repetition count is not a learning objective. Keep practice observations separate from assessment outcomes and mastery states.

Sources:
- [Queiroz, Foletto & López-Íñiguez (2025), PRISMA scoping review](https://journals.sagepub.com/doi/10.1177/03057356251348414)
- [Wang et al. (2025), systematic review and meta-analysis](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC12734040/)
- [How, Tan & Miksza (2022), PRISMA review of music practice](https://journals.sagepub.com/doi/10.1177/10298649211005531)

### 3.2 Assessment needs multiple criteria and level-sensitive descriptors

A 2024 PRISMA systematic review examined 20 papers and 26 solo-performance assessment systems. Across those systems, evaluation commonly included technical, interpretative/expressive and musical-feature dimensions such as pitch, rhythm and intonation. It notes that detailed rubrics can make expectations and feedback clearer, but also that assessment systems often fail to differentiate criteria by learner stage. It highlights the subjectivity of performance judgements and the risk of overemphasizing pitch/tempo accuracy while neglecting sound quality and interpretation.

Other rubric research shows that analytic criteria can improve feedback and consistency, but reliability is not automatic. Rater training, clear descriptors, fit-for-purpose criteria and periodic calibration matter.

**GuitarLab implication:** Define two or more skill-specific criteria per skill, but do not publish universal cut scores yet. Criteria should be adapted by proficiency level, musical style and task. A bend might have pitch-target, sustain and release dimensions; phrasing might assess timing, dynamics, articulation and use of space. Subjective outcomes need teacher/expert review or carefully designed reflective evidence; they should not be presented as objective automated scores.

Sources:
- [Moura et al. (2024), Solo music performance assessment criteria: a systematic review](https://www.frontiersin.org/journals/psychology/articles/10.3389/fpsyg.2024.1467434/full)
- [Álvarez-Díaz et al. (2021), design and validation of a musical-performance rubric](https://journals.sagepub.com/doi/10.1177/0255761420936443)
- [Latimer, Bergee & Cohen (2010), reliability and pedagogical utility of a weighted rubric](https://doi.org/10.1177/0022429410369836)

### 3.3 Immediate technology feedback is not proof of durable learning

A systematic review published 13 October 2026 examined technology-mediated feedback in instrumental practice. Its synthesis set contained 27 records (26 in descriptive synthesis), mostly piano and bowed-string studies. Only three reports described later-session retention and two tested different pieces; assessment conditions did not consistently establish performance without feedback. Heterogeneous designs and outcomes prevented a pooled estimate or technology ranking.

A music-specific study of practice variability found that how practice is varied can affect the acquisition and transfer of motor and timing skills differently. This argues against one simplistic “always randomize” rule: practice variability should serve the target, and its transfer effect should be tested.

**GuitarLab implication:** Any future tuner, pitch evaluator, rhythm scorer or real-time technique feedback must state exactly what signal it measures and under what conditions. A good in-session score should be labelled practice feedback, not mastery. Add delayed no-feedback checks and a transfer task in a different key, tempo, phrase or musical context before claiming reliable retention. Fade prompts or feedback over time when appropriate, and keep a learner's own listening/reflection in the loop.

Sources:
- [Zhang & Liu (2026), systematic review of technology-mediated feedback](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC13710021/)
- [Caramiaux et al. (2018), practice variability and motor/timing skill learning](https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/pmc/articles/PMC5832267/)
- [McKay et al. (2022), meta-analysis of reduced relative feedback frequency](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.psychsport.2022.102165)

### 3.4 Curriculum progression should include authentic musicianship

NAfME's 2014 Music Standards organize musical learning around Creating, Performing and Responding, with Connecting embedded through the processes. Its Model Cornerstone Assessments provide adaptable tasks and rubrics intended to measure learning through authentic demonstrations. Guitar-specific standards and best-practice guidance also support well-sequenced instruction, reading both notation and tablature, repertoire across styles/periods, tuning knowledge and fretboard logic.

**GuitarLab implication:** Skill IDs can remain narrow assessable capabilities, while lesson outcomes and capstones integrate several skills in authentic musical tasks. A capstone should not be a bag of isolated drills: ask the learner to create, perform, respond to a recording or peer, and explain musical choices where appropriate.

Sources:
- [NAfME — Standards](https://nafme.org/publications-resources/standards/)
- [NAfME — Model Cornerstone Assessments](https://nafme.org/resource/music-model-cornerstone-assessments/)
- [Guitar Foundation of America — Best Practices](https://www.guitarfoundation.org/page/BestPrac)

### 3.5 Product scan: useful interaction patterns, not efficacy evidence

Public guitar-learning products commonly expose staged learning paths, guided practice, practice workouts/jams, recording or expert feedback, visible milestones, and flexible learner-selected goals. These are useful product-design patterns, but vendor descriptions do not establish that a particular method improves learning outcomes.

Examples reviewed:
- [Guitar Master curriculum](https://guitarmaster.fr/en/curriculum/) — skills linked to exercises and a learner-facing practice compass.
- [Pickup Music](https://www.pickupmusic.com/guitar) — pathways, workouts, guided jams and expert feedback.
- [Fretello Learn Path](https://fretello.com/news/appupdate/fretello-learn-path/) — separates learning units from practice units.
- [TrueFire Beginner Guitar Method](https://truefire.com/learn/beginner-guitar-method) — lesson sets, targeted assignments and genre-based application.
- [Fender Play instructor guide](https://play-instructor.fender.com/hc/en-us/articles/13997465838477-Get-Started-Here) — a Path > Level > Course > Lesson hierarchy.

## 4. What the current GuitarLab data says

A source-derived scan of the three authored lesson datasets found:
- 152 lessons and 34 distinct skill IDs.
- 341 lesson-to-skill associations (not 341 distinct skills).
- `harmony.open-chords` appears in 33 lessons across Year 1, Year 2 and Year 3+.
- `performance.recording`, `performance.recovery`, and `repertoire.song-learning` each appear in 29 lessons, with the same broad spread across review/capstone lessons.
- `lead.phrasing` appears in 22 lessons; `lead.pentatonic` in 16.

These frequencies are not errors by themselves. The open-chord frequency, for example, is a signal to inspect whether the skill is too broad or reused on lessons where open chords are only incidental. The repeated recording/recovery/repertoire trio may be intentional for review lessons, but the checkpoint should show what is actually being assessed.

A strict lexical evidence scan found at least one assigned skill with no directly matching phrase in 64 lessons. This is a **screening signal only**: synonyms, implicit demonstration, and musical context can all defeat keyword matching. It does not establish that 64 lessons have wrong mappings. It supports a human review queue rather than automated deletion.

Five previously flagged lessons were inspected in full:
- Three mapping sets are provisionally supported by actual lesson content.
- Year 1 Week 2 and Week 20 have harmony tags with no supporting lesson evidence located.
- See [focused mapping review](M7_2_FLAGGED_MAPPING_REVIEW.md).

## 5. Recommended improvements and priority

| Priority | Improvement | Why it matters | Safe first implementation |
|---|---|---|---|
| P0 | Draft assessment profiles for all 34 existing skills | Catalogue existence is not a rubric; criteria need to be observable and skill-specific | Add a separate versioned draft profile file; no scoring or mastery changes |
| P0 | Evidence-based review of all 341 mappings | Broad or incidental tags can distort recommendations | Continue manual lesson-by-lesson register; keep machine-generated flags advisory |
| P1 | Plan–practice–reflect support in Practice Studio | Develop the learner's practice skill, not just guitar skill | Add optional task goal, strategy, reflection and next-step fields; keep observations distinct from assessment |
| P1 | Retention and transfer checks | In-session performance can be feedback-dependent | Add delayed no-feedback and changed-context assessment templates |
| P1 | Level-/style-sensitive rubric descriptors | The same criterion looks different for beginners and advanced learners or across styles | Add descriptor bands only after curriculum-owner review and piloting |
| P2 | Authentic integrated capstones | Preserve creating, performing, responding and connecting | Extend existing capstone tasks; do not make every criterion automatic |
| P2 | Assessment quality evaluation | Rubrics need reliability and fairness evidence | Pilot a sample with multiple reviewers; examine disagreement and refine descriptors |

## 6. Proposed assessment-profile contract

The separate draft file `content/curriculum/skill-assessment-profiles.json` should:
- reference the exact stable skill IDs in the v1.0.0 catalogue;
- include skill-specific observable criteria and suitable evidence modes;
- specify a delayed retention probe and a transfer probe;
- identify where human judgement is required;
- leave all universal score thresholds and automatic mastery decisions unapproved;
- carry its own version and draft status;
- never rewrite a learner's historical assessment record.

The data contract is intentionally separate from `skill-catalog.json`: skill identity, lesson mapping and assessment procedure are different concerns. Draft profiles are guidance for curriculum review, not live assessment rules.

## 7. Guardrails

- Do not add new skill IDs merely to mirror every exercise or musical phrase.
- Do not equate lesson completion, repetition counts, clean-repetition self-reports or current-session feedback with mastery.
- Do not infer a mastery state from one unvalidated score.
- Do not make a subjective musical judgement look machine-objective.
- Do not require one posture, genre, tone, or expressive interpretation as universal.
- Do not make automatic curriculum mapping changes from string matching.
- Version all assessment-profile changes and keep learner evidence linked to the catalogue release used at the time.
